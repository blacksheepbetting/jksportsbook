The Golden State Warriors: The NBA’s Most Dangerous Dark Horse?

With Stephen Curry’s championship pedigree, Jimmy Butler’s postseason dominance, and the unexpected emergence of Yaxel Lendeborg, Golden State may have the ingredients for one last championship run. But all the pieces must fall in the right places at the right time.

Brandon “BK” Klein / 12 min read / November 19, 2026

In a Western Conference loaded with championship contenders, the Golden State Warriors find themselves in a recently familiar position.

Once the NBA’s most feared dynasty, Golden State is no longer the overwhelming favorite it was during its championship years. Instead, coming off of 3 straight seasons in the play-in tournament, the Warriors enter the 2026–27 season facing legitimate questions about their age, durability, roster construction, and ability to compete with the league’s emerging young powers.

But perhaps this is exactly what makes Golden State such an intriguing dark horse.

Championships aren’t won in the winter, and regular-season standings don’t always tell the complete story of a team’s postseason potential. For the Warriors, the difference between an early playoff exit and a legitimate championship run may come down to two names: Stephen Curry, and Jimmy Butler.

Curry remains the engine of Golden State’s offensive identity, possessing the shooting ability, gravity, and championship experience to transform a playoff series. Butler, meanwhile, brings an entirely different dimension: physicality, defensive versatility, and a proven ability to elevate his game when the stakes are highest.

Together, they represent a potentially dangerous combination of offensive brilliance and postseason toughness. But the larger question isn’t simply whether Golden State possesses enough talent to compete.

It’s whether the Warriors can get healthy, continue developing their emerging young talent, and position themselves to play their best basketball when the postseason arrives.

The Warriors may not possess the youth, depth, or regular-season consistency of the NBA’s leading contenders. What they do possess, however, is something that cannot be easily measured: championship experience, a proven offensive system, and veteran leadership capable of changing the direction of a playoff series.

That doesn’t make Golden State a championship favorite.

It makes them a team nobody should be particularly eager to face in a seven-game series.

Stephen Curry: The Championship Equation Still Begins With No. 30

At 38 years old, Stephen Curry remains the single most important factor in Golden State’s championship aspirations.

No player in NBA history has influenced opposing defenses the way Curry continues to do. His deep shooting range forces defenders to extend well beyond the three-point line, creating opportunities for teammates through movement, spacing, and defensive attention that can’t be measured by box scores.

Even as the Warriors have evolved around him, Curry’s presence remains the foundation of their offense.

But the concern surrounding Curry is no longer simply whether he can perform at an elite level. It’s whether Golden State can preserve that level of performance throughout the regular season without placing an unrealistic burden on its 38 year old superstar.

His recent oblique injury is a perfect example of the balance between staying competitive and qualifying for the post season, and maintaining his health so he’s ready for a playoff push.

Without Curry, Golden State loses more than its leading scorer. The Warriors lose the player whose presence creates the spacing and offensive rhythm that their system depends on.

Defenses can become more aggressive against secondary scoring options, passing lanes become increasingly difficult to exploit, and the team’s offensive execution can deteriorate.

That dependence creates a significant challenge.

For Golden State to become a legitimate championship threat, Curry cannot be expected to single-handedly carry the offense through an exhausting regular season and then reproduce that same workload across four consecutive playoff series.

The Warriors need him healthy, rested, and capable of delivering his best basketball when it matters most.

And that is where Jimmy Butler becomes so important.

Jimmy Butler: The Missing Ingredient in Golden State’s Championship Aspirations

Throughout his career, Butler has demonstrated an ability to thrive in the slower, more physical environment of the NBA playoffs.

His willingness to attack mismatches, draw fouls, create offense in isolation, and defend multiple positions makes him particularly valuable.

That versatility addresses one of the challenges Golden State has faced as its championship core has aged.

Curry’s movement-based offense can be devastating, but playoff defenses are designed to take away a team’s greatest strengths. When opponents switch aggressively, pressure the perimeter, and force possessions into difficult late-clock situations, having another player capable of creating quality scoring opportunities becomes essential.

A healthy Butler could provide precisely that.

His presence would also reduce the pressure on Curry to initiate every meaningful offensive possession, allowing Golden State to diversify its attack and potentially preserve Curry’s energy throughout a demanding postseason.

But there is an obvious obstacle.

Butler’s recovery from ACL surgery introduces significant uncertainty about his availability and effectiveness.

Returning to the court is one thing. Regaining the explosiveness, lateral mobility, conditioning, and confidence necessary to perform at an elite postseason level is another.

Golden State’s championship ceiling depends not simply on Butler returning, but on whether he can once again become the physical, two-way presence that made him such a historically dominant playoff opponent. He carried multiple teams with arguably less talent to finals appearances when he was with the Heat, so there is no question that their odds skyrocket with him available.

If he can regain his form, the Warriors suddenly possess a combination of offensive creativity, defensive toughness, and postseason experience that could make them considerably more dangerous than their regular-season record will suggest.

If he cannot, Golden State may find itself asking Curry to shoulder more responsibility than is realistic at this stage of his career. Luckily for the warriors, they may actually have another answer waiting in the wings.

The Unexpected Emergence of Yaxel Lendeborg

Perhaps the most encouraging development of Golden State’s young season has been the emergence of rookie Yaxel Lendeborg.

Lendeborg, the current betting favorite for ROY, has introduced an entirely new element into the team’s championship equation.

The 24-year-old forward has quickly established himself as Golden State’s second-leading scorer behind Curry, providing a combination of size, scoring versatility, rebounding, and offensive production that the Warriors desperately need.

His emergence is particularly significant because it addresses one of the most important questions surrounding an aging championship contender: where will the next generation of offensive production come from?

For Golden State, Lendeborg may represent at least part of that answer.

Rather than relying exclusively on its veteran stars, the Warriors now have a young player capable of assuming meaningful offensive responsibility while contributing in multiple areas of the game.

His development could become especially valuable when Curry returns to full strength.

With opposing defenses forced to account for Curry’s shooting gravity, Lendeborg should have opportunities to attack favorable matchups, exploit defensive rotations, and operate within an offense that creates space for secondary options.

The potential return of Butler adds another intriguing dimension.

A lineup featuring Curry, Butler, and Lendeborg could offer Golden State a more balanced offensive approach, combining perimeter shooting, physical shot creation, and youthful athleticism.

Of course, early regular-season success does not guarantee postseason effectiveness.

Opposing teams will eventually adjust their defensive schemes, challenge Lendeborg’s decision-making, and test his ability to produce consistently under greater pressure.

But one thing is becoming increasingly clear.

Golden State may have discovered a young offensive weapon at precisely the moment its veteran core needs additional support.

And that development could influence more than just the team’s on-court performance.

It could also shape the decisions Golden State makes before the trade deadline.

Brandin Podziemski and the Value of Patience at the Trade Deadline

For a franchise operating within the closing championship window of its aging superstars, the temptation to make an aggressive midseason trade can be difficult to resist.

But Golden State’s situation demands something more calculated.

Brandin Podziemski’s recently signed five-year, $110 million contract extension represents a substantial investment in the Warriors’ future.

His versatility, rebounding, playmaking, and developing perimeter shooting make him a valuable contributor, even if his offensive effectiveness and his overall ceiling remains a subject of debate.

Meanwhile, Lendeborg’s rapid emergence has provided Golden State with another potential foundational piece, one whose early offensive production could reduce the urgency to pursue additional high-volume scoring.

Through his first 12 NBA games, Lendeborg is averaging 21.7 points, 7.4 rebounds, and 3.7 assists, shooting 51.8% from the field and 42.6% from three-point range.

Meanwhile, Podziemski ended 2025–26 averaging 13.8 points, 5.1 rebounds, and 3.7 assists.

That development creates an interesting organizational dilemma.

Should the Warriors continue investing in their young core, or consider moving valuable assets if an established veteran becomes available who could meaningfully improve their chances at one last championship run.

The answer should not be an automatic commitment to either direction.

If the right veteran becomes available — particularly one who addresses a clear weakness in perimeter defense, physicality, or frontcourt versatility — the Warriors should be willing to explore their options.

That doesn’t mean Podziemski should be actively shopped, nor does it mean sacrificing valuable young talent for a marginal improvement.

It means recognizing that Golden State is attempting to accomplish two difficult objectives simultaneously: remaining competitive in the present while developing the foundation of its future.

The emergence of Lendeborg makes that balancing act somewhat easier, but it does not eliminate the need for careful decision-making.

Ultimately, the Warriors shouldn’t make a trade simply because their championship window is closing.

They should make a trade only if it meaningfully improves their chances of winning while that window remains open.

The Western Conference: Why the Road Won’t Be Easy

The Western Conference presents a demanding combination of elite talent, athleticism, defensive versatility, and increasingly sophisticated offensive systems.

Younger contenders possess advantages in speed, durability, and the ability to maintain high-intensity play throughout an extended postseason.

For a Warriors roster built around veteran stars, those advantages cannot be dismissed.

Golden State would likely need to overcome opponents capable of applying relentless defensive pressure, controlling the glass, and exploiting the physical limitations of an older roster.

The Warriors’ margin for error may be considerably smaller than that of teams with greater depth or fewer health concerns.

However, experience, coaching adjustments, execution in close games, and the ability to identify and exploit individual matchups can dramatically influence the outcome of a seven-game series.

Golden State possesses several advantages in those areas.

Steve Kerr has spent more than a decade directing one of basketball’s most successful offensive systems. Draymond Green remains an experienced and effective defensive anchor, capable of recognizing opposing actions and coordinating adjustments in real time.

For the Warriors to seriously challenge the West’s strongest teams, they must combine that experience with reliable secondary scoring, and consistent defensive execution. They also need to maintain the health and availability of their stars.

A healthy Curry and Butler would provide a foundation.

Lendeborg’s continued development could raise the ceiling.

The supporting cast would still need to provide the consistency necessary to turn that potential into actual playoff victories.

What Must Go Right for a Championship Run?

Golden State’s path to another championship is not impossible, but it is absolutely conditional.

First and foremost, Curry must return to full strength and remain healthy throughout the postseason. Without him operating near his established level, the Warriors simply lack the offensive foundation necessary to sustain a deep playoff run.

Second, Butler must recover sufficiently to provide the physicality, defensive versatility, and secondary shot creation that make his game so valuable in postseason basketball.

Third, Lendeborg must continue developing into a dependable offensive contributor. Golden State cannot rely entirely on its veteran stars, particularly against opponents capable of forcing the ball out of Curry’s hands.

Fourth, the supporting cast must deliver consistent production. Players such as Podziemski, Green, and the team’s other rotation contributors will have to make meaningful impacts without requiring the offense to revolve around them.

Finally, the organization must approach the trade deadline with discipline. An opportunity to improve the roster should be considered, but forcing a trade at the expense of valuable young talent could create more problems than it solves.

None of these conditions guarantees a championship.

Collectively, however, they offer a plausible explanation for how Golden State could become substantially more dangerous than its early-season performance indicates. As shown by their 6 game win streak during late October and early November.

Final Verdict: A Dangerous Dark Horse, Not Yet a Contender

The Golden State Warriors are far from being considered a threat to win the 2026–27 NBA championship. In fact as of right now they are not even in the top 10 of most lists.

Their age, injury concerns, and dependence on Stephen Curry create legitimate questions about their ability to make a deep post season run.

Jimmy Butler’s recovery adds another layer of uncertainty, while the team’s younger contributors still have much to prove against elite competition.

But dismissing Golden State entirely would overlook the factors that make this team so intriguing.

Curry remains capable of transforming an offense in ways no player in NBA history ever has. Butler, when healthy and in form, offers a skill set alongside the mental toughness that is extremely valuable in postseason basketball.

Draymond Green remains an elite passer, an all important cog in the warriors offensive schemes, and the undeniably effective leader of the defense.

Lendeborg’s unexpected breakout provides a promising source of youthful production, while Podziemski and the rest of the supporting cast offer opportunities for continued development.

And behind it all stands an organization with championship experience, an established system, and an understanding of what it takes to win when the stakes are highest.

The Warriors don’t need to recreate the overwhelming dominance of their dynasty years to become a legitimate postseason threat. This team would be a lot closer to the 2022 championship team.

They need to get healthy, develop chemistry, establish a reliable rotation, and find a way to enter the playoffs with a full, healthy roster, playing their best basketball.

There are younger teams, deeper teams, and teams whose regular-season performances may ultimately justify considerably greater expectations.

But there is a difference between being the most likely team to win a championship and being a team capable of making an unexpected run.

Golden State belongs in that second conversation.

The Warriors’ championship window may be narrowing, but it has not necessarily closed.

And if Curry returns healthy, Butler rediscovers his postseason form, and Lendeborg continues his rapid development, the rest of the Western Conference may discover that the Warriors have one more dangerous run left in them.

Because Golden State doesn’t need to look like a championship favorite in November.

It needs to become a championship contender in April.
