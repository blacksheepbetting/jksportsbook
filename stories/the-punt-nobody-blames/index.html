The Punt Nobody Blames

College football celebrates discipline when coaches punt. But who benefits from that caution—and who pays for the missed opportunity?

Corey Becker / 4 min read / November 25, 2026

College football has a remarkable public-relations operation for giving up the ball.

Send out the punter, and the vocabulary arrives automatically: discipline, patience, field position. The coach trusts his defense. He understands the moment. He refuses to panic.

Apparently, surrendering possession comes with a complimentary leadership seminar.

But before we congratulate another millionaire coach for playing it safe, somebody should ask what, exactly, he’s making safer.

His team’s chances of winning? Or his postgame press conference?

Those interests can pull in different directions. Fans deserve to know when they do.

A failed fourth-down attempt leaves fingerprints. Everybody sees the runner get stuffed. Everybody remembers where the opponent took over. The coach owns one obvious decision that can be clipped, replayed and screamed about until next Saturday.

An unnecessary punt can bury its cost beneath ten more minutes of football. The opponent drives. Possessions disappear. An opportunity slips away without producing one convenient frame for the indictment.

That asymmetry is a gift to anyone more worried about looking reckless than making the best decision.

College football’s culture is perfectly equipped to wrap that gift in tradition.

We worship toughness. We celebrate imposing your will. Programs sell recruits on dominant offensive lines and unstoppable athletes.

Then fourth-and-short arrives, and suddenly the most physically intimidating operation in the stadium retreats behind a lesson about patience.

If your offense has a meaningful advantage in that situation, explain why giving the ball away is the better play. Show the reasoning. Wearing a headset doesn’t make the question disrespectful.

The case against automatic conservatism has been around for decades. In a 2006 study, economist David Romer found that NFL teams’ fourth-down decisions were systematically more conservative than his analysis suggested they should be to maximize their chances of winning. High salaries and professional expertise hadn’t eliminated the problem. Romer, Do Firms Maximize? Evidence from Professional Football

That history undercuts the easy claim that NFL coaches have always been enlightened mathematical operators. Football’s attachment to respectable-looking mistakes extends across both levels.

College football has made progress, too. CFB/LAB’s compilation shows teams attempting conversions on roughly 27.4 percent of fourth-down decisions in 2025, up from 19.5 percent in 2017. The sport is changing. CFB/LAB fourth-down analysis

Good. Keep pushing.

Going for it more often is a miserable standard for a profession paid to find advantages. The standard should be choosing well in the situations a team actually faces.

Conversion leaderboards can’t settle that question. A coach can attempt only the easiest fourth downs and produce a beautiful percentage while leaving valuable opportunities untouched. Another can inherit a pile of desperate late-game situations and finish with numbers that look terrible.

A leaderboard needs context. So does a punt.

The same scrutiny should apply to powerhouse programs whose recruiting advantages can cushion strategic mistakes. When a superior roster wins anyway, the final score can conceal opportunities the coach wasted.

Winning with better players doesn’t automatically validate every decision along the way.

That should be obvious. Somehow, asking a celebrated coach to defend a punt can still sound like sacrilege.

A model deserves scrutiny, too. Injuries, weather, personnel and the matchup matter. Researchers have also found substantial uncertainty in fourth-down recommendations—more than popular presentations often acknowledge. Brill, Yurko and Wyner, Analytics, Have Some Humility

Fine. Explain those factors.

Explain why your particular situation changes the calculation. Explain why punting gives your team the better chance. A serious football argument should survive questions about football.

Spare fans the ceremonial nonsense that treats every aggressive failure as coaching malpractice and every conservative surrender as evidence of maturity.

That standard practically begs coaches to protect themselves.

Athletic directors should evaluate the choice using the information available before the snap. Fans should demand the same. A worthwhile fourth-down attempt can fail. An indefensible punt can be followed by a defensive touchdown. Neither outcome changes the quality of the original decision.

Your loyalty should never become camouflage for weak reasoning.

You give the program your Saturdays, your attention and, often, your money. The person collecting the enormous paycheck owes you more than an explanation built from phrases everybody has heard since childhood.

He owes you a serious effort to maximize the chance of winning—even when the best decision risks making him look foolish.

Every punt deserves an explanation.

Stop handing out credit for courage to men whose safest move is giving the ball away.
